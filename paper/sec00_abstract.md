# From Connectome to Computation: Structural Principles of the Drosophila Brain and What They Do in Artificial Networks

## Abstract
The whole-brain connectome of the fruit fly offers 134,181 neurons and 2.7 million strong connections (at least 5 synapses) as a test bed for a simple question: is the wiring unusual in ways that could matter for computation, and does imitating those unusual features help an artificial network? We counted directed three-node motifs against three null models, one of which holds anatomical compartments fixed. Mutual pairs (z = +1,355 against the compartment-preserving null) and empty triads are strongly enriched, while single edges and open chains are suppressed (z from -280 to -1,355). The feedforward loop is enriched (z = +132) and concentrates in the visual pathway. The cyclic triad is not a motif once compartments are held fixed. The graph is modular (Q = 0.67 to 0.69), shows no rich club, and its hubs are less interconnected than their degrees predict. Four-node patterns follow the triad rule in an exploratory census of eight neuropils: median enrichment rises from -4.7 to +9.9 as mutual pairs increase. We translated these structures into sparse feed-forward networks and compared them in about 2,400 preregistered, seed-paired training runs against matched random, pruned, dense and convolutional networks. Within matched sparse networks the connectome-derived mask retains accuracy under unit ablation more gradually than a random mask (retention-curve area 0.570 against 0.430, 15 of 20 seeds, p = 8.5e-4). That effect does not extend to dense or convolutional networks, which degrade less, or to the same mask imposed on a pretrained network. The connectome mask never beat a random mask of equal budget on accuracy, so the structural findings stand as a description of the brain and not as an architecture that outperforms existing baselines.
